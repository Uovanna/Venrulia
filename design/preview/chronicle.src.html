The fight is a chronicle being written down

Vellum, carrying the Ledger's conviction about the log — and answering the texture question, which turned out to have a rule behind it rather than a yes or no.

Texture

Yes — but only ever behind the type, never under it

Texture doesn't overcrowd an interface by existing. It overcrowds when its contrast competes with the stroke width of the text sitting on it. So the rule this system follows is a spatial one: the page ground is textured, and every raised surface — the chronicle block, panels, the enemy's frame — paints a flat colour over it. Prose always sits on an even bed; the material is visible around it.

Both layers are generated with an SVG turbulence filter as a data URI. No image request, no tile seam, no payload, and it's painted once — which matters for a game people leave running.

Fibre — shipped

Fibre, plus staining

High-frequency grain at 5.5% (day) and 7.5% (night), with three off-centre stains and a vignette so it isn't uniform. Reads as material at arm's length and disappears at reading distance — which is exactly what real parchment does.

Loud — rejected

What overcrowding looks like

Lower frequency, six times the amplitude. Now the grain is the same scale as the letterforms and starts eating the small mono figures. This is the failure mode you were asking about, and it's a question of amplitude, not of whether to have texture.

One consequence worth knowing: because texture lives only on the ground, any surface that needs to hold dense small text — inventory grids, the auction house, tooltips — will automatically be flat. That's a constraint the system enforces for you rather than a discipline you'd have to remember.

The screen

Prose in the body, quantities in the margin

The Ledger's conviction was that the log should dominate and its numbers should form a scannable column. Vellum's voice is prose. Those look opposed until you remember what a real ledger looks like: sentences in the body, figures ruled off in the margin. The chronicle keeps its grammar and the numbers line up, because that's how the source material was actually written.

Colour in the log is doing grammar, not decoration. Red is your own hand — the rubrication a scribe used for the actor's deeds. Brown is what's done to you. Green is restoration, gold is spoils. There are no other colours in the chronicle.

Day · vellum

Night · candlelight

Night is not the day palette inverted. An inverted parchment is grey, and grey has nothing to do with this game. It's the same page read by candlelight: a warm umber-black ground, ink that becomes aged bone, and a rubric that warms to an ember so it still reads as "your hand" against a dark surface. Every colour clears WCAG AA against both grounds in both themes — measured, not eyeballed.

Icons

One hand, not one hundred and sixty-five

I counted the emoji in the live build before designing anything: 165 distinct glyphs across 886 occurrences. I told you "roughly 60" earlier — that was wrong, and it changes the size of the job, so it's worth saying plainly.

Every glyph below is stroke-only on a 24-grid, inherits currentColor, and is roughened by a single shared turbulence filter that pushes each stroke about a pixel off its true line. That's the trick: what reads as "drawn by a person" is consistency of error, not quantity of it — so the wobble comes from one place instead of being faked path by path. Strokes overshoot their corners slightly, because a drawn corner is two strokes that cross, never two that meet.
